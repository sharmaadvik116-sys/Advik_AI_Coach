# Image Interpretation in Remote Sensing

## What Is Image Interpretation?

Image interpretation is the process of examining a remote sensing image and using visual and scientific clues to understand what the image represents.

A student may need to identify:

- What type of surface is shown
- What objects or features are present
- How different materials appear
- Whether something has changed
- What sensor or wavelength information is being displayed

Image interpretation combines observation with knowledge of how different materials interact with electromagnetic energy.

---

# Important Image Interpretation Clues

When interpreting an image, look at:

- Color
- Brightness
- Tone
- Shape
- Size
- Texture
- Pattern
- Location
- Association
- Context

Not every image uses all of these clues in the same way.

---

## Color

Color can help distinguish different features.

In a natural-color image:

- Vegetation may appear green
- Water may appear blue or dark
- Roads and buildings may appear gray or brown

However, the appearance depends on the image and the materials being observed.

Always check what type of imagery you are viewing before interpreting colors.

---

# Natural Color Imagery

Natural-color imagery is designed to look similar to what a human observer would see.

It commonly uses visible:

- Red
- Green
- Blue

spectral bands.

Natural-color imagery can make familiar surface features easier to recognize.

---

# False Color Imagery

False-color imagery assigns spectral bands to display colors in a way that may not match natural human vision.

This can make certain features easier to distinguish.

For example, near-infrared information can be displayed using a visible color channel.

Vegetation may therefore appear in colors that are different from natural green.

### Important Rule

Never assume that a color has the same meaning in every remote sensing image.

First determine:

1. Which sensor produced the image?
2. Which spectral bands are being displayed?
3. How were the bands assigned to display colors?
4. What does the legend indicate?

---

# Grayscale Imagery

A grayscale image uses different shades of gray to represent measured values.

Generally:

- Darker pixels represent lower displayed values
- Brighter pixels represent higher displayed values

However, the exact interpretation depends on what quantity the image represents.

For example, a grayscale image could represent:

- Reflected energy
- Thermal measurements
- Radar backscatter
- Another measured quantity

Always identify the data source before interpreting brightness.

---

# Thermal Imagery

Thermal infrared imagery represents information related to thermal radiation.

Different surface temperatures can produce different measured thermal signals.

Thermal imagery can help identify patterns such as:

- Warmer and cooler surfaces
- Urban heat patterns
- Water temperature differences
- Wildfire effects
- Surface temperature changes

The displayed colors in a thermal image are not necessarily the actual colors visible to the human eye.

---

# Radar Imagery

Radar imagery is produced from returned radar signals.

Radar images can look very different from visible-light images.

Radar backscatter can be affected by:

- Surface roughness
- Surface moisture
- Material properties
- Geometry
- Radar wavelength
- Viewing conditions

A bright radar return does not automatically mean an object is visually bright.

The meaning of brightness depends on the radar data.

---

# Elevation Displays

Elevation data can be displayed using colors, shading, or contour lines.

For example:

- Higher elevations may be displayed using one range of colors.
- Lower elevations may be displayed using another range.
- Shaded relief can make terrain appear three-dimensional.

Always check the legend to determine what the colors represent.

---

# Contour Maps

Contour lines connect locations having the same value.

When contour lines represent elevation:

- Each line represents a particular elevation.
- Closely spaced lines generally indicate steeper terrain.
- Widely spaced lines generally indicate gentler terrain.

The contour interval tells you the difference in elevation between adjacent contour lines.

---

# Classified Maps

A classified map assigns pixels or areas to categories.

Examples include:

- Forest
- Water
- Urban
- Cropland
- Grassland
- Barren land

Different colors or symbols may represent different classes.

Always use the legend to determine what each class means.

---

# Image Interpretation Process

A useful process is:

### Step 1: Identify the image type

Ask:

Is this:

- Natural color?
- False color?
- Grayscale?
- Thermal?
- Radar?
- Elevation?
- Classified?

---

### Step 2: Check the legend

Determine what:

- Colors
- Symbols
- Brightness
- Values

represent.

---

### Step 3: Identify the sensor or data source

If the information is provided, determine which sensor or dataset produced the image.

Different sensors measure different portions of the electromagnetic spectrum or different physical properties.

---

### Step 4: Look for patterns

Examine:

- Shape
- Size
- Texture
- Pattern
- Location
- Brightness
- Color

---

### Step 5: Use scientific knowledge

Ask how the material should interact with electromagnetic energy.

For example:

Vegetation often has strong reflectance in the near-infrared region.

Water often has relatively low reflectance in many near-infrared wavelengths.

---

### Step 6: Compare locations

If the question asks you to compare two areas, look for differences in:

- Color
- Brightness
- Texture
- Spectral response
- Temperature
- Radar backscatter
- Elevation

---

### Step 7: State the evidence

Do not simply say:

"The area is vegetation."

Instead explain the evidence.

For example:

"The area is likely vegetation because it has the expected spectral response in the displayed bands."

The exact evidence depends on the image.

---

# Image Interpretation and Spectral Information

Different materials interact with electromagnetic radiation differently.

This creates different spectral responses.

For example:

### Vegetation

Often reflects strongly in near-infrared wavelengths.

### Water

Often has low reflectance in many near-infrared wavelengths.

### Bare soil

Can have different reflectance patterns depending on:

- Moisture
- Mineral composition
- Surface condition

### Urban materials

Buildings, roads, and other surfaces can have different spectral and thermal responses.

These differences help remote sensing systems distinguish materials.

---

# Comparing Two Images

When comparing images of the same location:

### First check the dates

Determine when each image was collected.

### Then check the sensor/data type

The images should be interpreted with their measurement characteristics in mind.

### Then compare features

Look for:

- New buildings
- Forest loss
- Flooding
- Burn scars
- Vegetation changes
- Water-level changes
- Surface changes

---

# Change Detection

Change detection involves comparing observations from different times to identify changes.

A simple example:

An area of forest appears in an earlier image.

In a later image, the same area appears to have been converted to another land-cover type.

This may indicate land-cover change.

The interpretation should be based on the available evidence.

---

# Common Interpretation Mistakes

## Mistake 1: Assuming every image uses natural colors

Not all remote sensing images look like photographs.

Always identify the display type.

---

## Mistake 2: Assuming red always means vegetation

Red may represent different information depending on how the image was created.

Check the band combination and legend.

---

## Mistake 3: Ignoring the legend

The legend provides important information about colors, symbols, or values.

---

## Mistake 4: Looking only at color

Texture, shape, pattern, location, and scientific context can also provide important evidence.

---

## Mistake 5: Confusing thermal brightness with visible brightness

A bright thermal pixel represents a measurement related to thermal radiation, not necessarily a surface that looks bright to the human eye.

### Thermal

### Radar

Shows information derived from returned radar signals.

### Elevation

Shows height or terrain information.

### Classified Map

Assigns areas to categories.

# Key Interpretation Strategy

Remember:

**Identify → Check legend → Identify data/sensor → Observe → Compare → Explain with evidence**

When answering an image interpretation question, do not rely only on what something "looks like."

Ask:

**What was measured?**

**How was it displayed?**

**What physical property explains the pattern?**
