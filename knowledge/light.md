# Light and Electromagnetic Radiation

## What Is Light?

Light is electromagnetic radiation that carries energy.

Remote sensing uses electromagnetic radiation to collect information about Earth's surface and atmosphere.

Different types of electromagnetic radiation have different wavelengths and frequencies.

---

## Electromagnetic Spectrum

The electromagnetic spectrum includes many types of electromagnetic radiation.

Important regions for remote sensing include:

- Ultraviolet (UV)
- Visible light
- Near-infrared (NIR)
- Shortwave infrared (SWIR)
- Thermal infrared

Wavelength generally increases as you move from shorter-wavelength radiation toward longer-wavelength radiation.

Frequency and wavelength are related.

Higher frequency corresponds to shorter wavelength.

Lower frequency corresponds to longer wavelength.

---

## Wavelength

Wavelength is the distance between corresponding points on consecutive waves.

It is commonly represented by the Greek letter lambda:

λ

Common units include:

- meters (m)
- micrometers (µm)
- nanometers (nm)

Remember:

1 micrometer = 1,000 nanometers

---

## Frequency

Frequency describes how many wave cycles occur per second.

Frequency is commonly represented by:

f

The unit of frequency is the hertz (Hz).

One hertz means one cycle per second.

---

## Wave Equation

The relationship between wavelength, frequency, and the speed of light is:

c = λf

Where:

- c = speed of light
- λ = wavelength
- f = frequency

For electromagnetic radiation traveling in a vacuum:

c ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s

### Example

Suppose an electromagnetic wave has a wavelength of:

λ = 600 nm

Convert nanometers to meters:

600 nm = 600 × 10⁻⁹ m

Then use:

f = c / λ

So:

f = (3.00 × 10⁸ m/s) / (600 × 10⁻⁹ m)

f ≈ 5.00 × 10¹⁴ Hz

---

## Reflection

Reflection occurs when electromagnetic radiation strikes a surface and bounces back.

Different surfaces reflect different amounts of energy.

For example:

- A bright surface may reflect a large amount of radiation.
- A dark surface may absorb more radiation and reflect less.

Remote sensing sensors can measure reflected energy to help identify surface materials.

---

## Refraction

Refraction is the bending of electromagnetic radiation when it moves from one medium into another because its speed changes.

For example, light changes direction when it passes from air into water.

The amount of bending depends on the properties of the materials.

---

## Snell's Law

Snell's law describes how radiation bends when it passes between materials.

n₁ sin(θ₁) = n₂ sin(θ₂)

Where:

- n₁ = refractive index of the first medium
- θ₁ = angle of incidence
- n₂ = refractive index of the second medium
- θ₂ = angle of refraction

Angles are measured relative to the normal line.

---

## Absorption

Absorption occurs when a material takes in electromagnetic energy.

Different materials absorb different wavelengths.

This is important in remote sensing because absorption patterns can help scientists identify materials.

The atmosphere also absorbs some wavelengths of electromagnetic radiation.

---

## Scattering

Scattering occurs when electromagnetic radiation changes direction because it interacts with particles or molecules.

Atmospheric particles can scatter radiation.

Scattering can affect remote sensing observations.

Different types of scattering can occur depending on the relationship between particle size and wavelength.

---

## Polarization

Polarization describes the orientation of the electric field of electromagnetic radiation.

Some remote sensing systems, especially radar systems, use information about polarization.

Different surfaces can change the polarization of returned signals in different ways.

---

## Irradiance

Irradiance describes the amount of radiant power received by a surface per unit area.

A common unit is:

W/m²

Irradiance is related to how much energy reaches a surface.

---

## Radiance

Radiance describes the amount of electromagnetic energy traveling in a particular direction from a source or surface, normalized by area and solid angle.

Radiance is an important quantity measured or derived in remote sensing.

---

## Inverse-Square Law

For a point source spreading energy uniformly, irradiance decreases with the square of the distance from the source.

The relationship is:

I ∝ 1/r²

or, when comparing two distances:

I₂ = I₁(r₁/r₂)²

Where:

- I₁ = irradiance at the first distance
- I₂ = irradiance at the second distance
- r₁ = first distance
- r₂ = second distance

### Important Idea

If the distance from the source doubles:

r₂ = 2r₁

Then:

I₂ = I₁(1/2)²

I₂ = I₁/4

So the irradiance becomes one-fourth as large.

---

## Wien's Displacement Law

Wien's displacement law relates the temperature of an object to the wavelength at which its thermal emission is strongest.

The equation is:

λmax = b/T

Where:

- λmax = wavelength of maximum emission
- b = Wien's displacement constant
- T = absolute temperature in kelvin (K)

The commonly used value of the constant is approximately:

b = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K

### Important Relationship

As temperature increases:

- peak wavelength decreases

As temperature decreases:

- peak wavelength increases

This relationship helps explain why thermal remote sensing can detect differences in surface temperature.

---

## Stefan-Boltzmann Law

The Stefan-Boltzmann law relates the total thermal energy emitted by an ideal blackbody to its temperature.

The equation is:

E = σT⁴

Where:

- E = emitted energy per unit area
- σ = Stefan-Boltzmann constant
- T = absolute temperature in kelvin

The Stefan-Boltzmann constant is approximately:

σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴)

### Important Relationship

Because temperature is raised to the fourth power, a relatively small change in temperature can produce a larger change in emitted energy.

---

## Blackbody

A blackbody is an idealized object that absorbs all electromagnetic radiation that reaches it and emits radiation according to its temperature.

Real objects are not perfect blackbodies, but the concept is useful for understanding thermal radiation.

---

## Why Light Matters in Remote Sensing

Remote sensing depends on how electromagnetic radiation interacts with Earth's surface and atmosphere.

A simplified sequence is:

1. Energy reaches a target.
2. The target reflects, absorbs, transmits, or emits energy.
3. A sensor detects some of the energy.
4. The recorded information becomes data.
5. The data can be processed into images or measurements.
6. Scientists interpret the information.

Different materials interact with different wavelengths in different ways.

This allows remote sensing instruments to distinguish between surface materials and conditions.
