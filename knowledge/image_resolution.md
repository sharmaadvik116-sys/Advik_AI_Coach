# Image Resolution in Remote Sensing

## What Is Image Resolution?

Resolution describes how much detail a remote sensing system can distinguish or record.

Remote sensing commonly uses four important types of resolution:

1. Spatial resolution
2. Temporal resolution
3. Radiometric resolution
4. Spectral resolution

These describe different characteristics of a sensor or image.

---

# Spatial Resolution

Spatial resolution describes the size of the area represented by each pixel on the ground.

It is commonly related to the pixel size or ground sampling distance.

For example:

If an image has a spatial resolution of 10 meters, one pixel represents approximately:

10 m × 10 m

of ground area.

### Important Idea

Smaller pixels generally allow smaller features to be distinguished.

For example:

A 10-meter image can generally show smaller features than a 30-meter image, assuming other factors are comparable.

### Remember

**Smaller pixel → more spatial detail**

**Larger pixel → less spatial detail**

---

# Temporal Resolution

Temporal resolution describes how frequently a sensor can observe the same area.

It is sometimes described using revisit time.

For example:

- A sensor that observes an area every 5 days has a shorter revisit interval than one that observes it every 16 days.
- More frequent observations can be useful for monitoring changes that happen quickly.

Temporal resolution is especially important when studying:

- Vegetation changes
- Floods
- Fires
- Storms
- Seasonal changes
- Other changing conditions

### Remember

**Shorter revisit time → more frequent observations**

---

# Radiometric Resolution

Radiometric resolution describes how finely a sensor can distinguish differences in the amount of detected electromagnetic energy.

A sensor with greater radiometric sensitivity can distinguish smaller differences in signal.

Digital images store measured values using digital numbers or similar measurements.

For example, an image with more possible intensity levels can represent finer differences in measured energy.

### Important Idea

Radiometric resolution is about:

**How finely can the sensor distinguish differences in signal intensity?**

It is different from spatial resolution.

---

# Spectral Resolution

Spectral resolution describes how finely a sensor can distinguish different wavelengths or wavelength ranges.

Sensors may measure electromagnetic radiation using different spectral bands.

A sensor with narrow spectral bands can distinguish smaller wavelength ranges than a sensor using very broad bands.

### Important Idea

Spectral resolution is about:

**How finely can the sensor distinguish different wavelengths?**

---

# Spectral Bands

A spectral band is a range of wavelengths measured by a sensor.

For example, a sensor might have separate bands for:

- Blue
- Green
- Red
- Near-infrared
- Shortwave infrared

Different materials reflect or emit different amounts of energy in different bands.

This allows scientists to use spectral information to study Earth's surface.

---

# Comparing the Four Resolutions

| Resolution | Main Question |
|---|---|
| Spatial | How much ground detail can I see? |
| Temporal | How often can I observe the area? |
| Radiometric | How finely can I distinguish signal intensity? |
| Spectral | How finely can I distinguish wavelengths? |

---

# Spatial vs. Temporal Resolution

These two are commonly confused.

### Spatial resolution

Concerns **detail in space**.

Example:

A 10-meter pixel versus a 30-meter pixel.

### Temporal resolution

Concerns **frequency in time**.

Example:

An observation every 5 days versus every 16 days.

### Easy way to remember

**Spatial = Where/detail**

**Temporal = When/how often**

---

# Spatial vs. Spectral Resolution

These are also different.

### Spatial resolution

Tells us how much ground each pixel represents.

### Spectral resolution

Tells us how finely the sensor separates wavelengths.

A sensor can have:

- High spatial resolution
- High spectral resolution
- Both
- Or different combinations depending on the instrument

---

# Radiometric vs. Spatial Resolution

Radiometric resolution is about differences in measured energy.

Spatial resolution is about the size of the area represented by a pixel.

For example:

Two images could have the same spatial resolution but different radiometric resolutions.

The pixels could represent the same ground area while the sensors distinguish different numbers or ranges of signal levels.

---

# Resolution Trade-Offs

Remote sensing systems are designed for particular purposes.

Improving one characteristic does not automatically mean every other characteristic improves.

Sensor design can involve trade-offs between:

- Spatial resolution
- Temporal resolution
- Spectral resolution
- Radiometric resolution
- Swath width
- Data volume

For example, collecting very detailed imagery over a large area can require more data and may affect how frequently the system can collect observations.

---

# Example

Imagine two satellite images of the same city.

### Image A

- 10-meter spatial resolution
- Observation every 10 days

### Image B

- 30-meter spatial resolution
- Observation every 2 days

Image A provides:

- More spatial detail

Image B provides:

- More frequent observations

Neither resolution is simply "better" for every scientific question.

The appropriate resolution depends on what you are trying to study.
