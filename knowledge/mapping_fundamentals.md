# Mapping Fundamentals in Remote Sensing

## What Is a Map?

A map is a visual representation of geographic information.

Remote sensing data can be displayed as maps or images to help scientists understand Earth's surface.

Maps can show information such as:

- Location
- Distance
- Direction
- Elevation
- Land cover
- Surface features
- Changes over time

---

## Map Displays

Remote sensing information can be displayed in different ways depending on what information needs to be studied.

Examples include:

- Satellite imagery
- Aerial imagery
- Topographic maps
- Elevation maps
- Classified maps
- Contour maps
- Radar imagery
- Thermal imagery

The type of display affects how information can be interpreted.

---

## Map Scale

Map scale describes the relationship between a distance on a map and the corresponding distance on the ground.

For example:

A map scale of:

1:100,000

means that:

1 unit on the map represents 100,000 of the same units on the ground.

For example:

1 centimeter on the map represents 100,000 centimeters on the ground.

---

## Large-Scale vs. Small-Scale Maps

### Large-Scale Map

A large-scale map shows a smaller geographic area with more detail.

Example:

A detailed map of a neighborhood.

### Small-Scale Map

A small-scale map shows a larger geographic area with less detail.

Example:

A map showing an entire country.

### Important Memory Trick

**Large scale → smaller area → more detail**

**Small scale → larger area → less detail**

---

## Map Orientation

Orientation tells the user how directions on the map relate to directions on Earth.

A north arrow is commonly used to show the direction of north.

The four main cardinal directions are:

- North
- South
- East
- West

Intermediate directions include:

- Northeast
- Northwest
- Southeast
- Southwest

---

## Legend

A map legend explains the symbols, colors, or patterns used on a map.

For example, a legend might show:

- Blue = water
- Green = vegetation
- Gray = urban area
- Brown = bare land

The exact meaning of a color or symbol depends on the map.

Always check the legend before interpreting a map.

---

## Coordinates

Coordinates are used to describe the location of a point on Earth.

Geographic coordinates commonly use:

- Latitude
- Longitude

### Latitude

Latitude describes how far north or south a location is from the Equator.

The Equator is:

0° latitude

Latitude ranges from:

90° South to 90° North

### Longitude

Longitude describes how far east or west a location is from the Prime Meridian.

The Prime Meridian is:

0° longitude

Longitude ranges from:

180° West to 180° East.

---

## Contour Maps

A contour map uses lines to represent locations with equal values.

For elevation maps, contour lines connect points having the same elevation.

Contour lines can help show the shape of terrain.

### Closely Spaced Contours

Closely spaced contour lines generally indicate a steep slope.

### Widely Spaced Contours

Widely spaced contour lines generally indicate a gentler slope.

---

## Elevation

Elevation describes the height of a location relative to a reference surface.

Elevation information can be represented using:

- Contour lines
- Digital elevation models
- Shaded relief
- Color-coded elevation maps

Remote sensing can be used to collect information that helps create elevation products.

---

## Digital Elevation Models

A Digital Elevation Model (DEM) is a digital representation of elevation over an area.

A DEM can be used to study:

- Terrain
- Mountains
- Valleys
- Slopes
- Drainage
- Elevation changes

DEM data can also support mapping and geographic analysis.

---

## Map Marginal Information

Marginal information is information provided around a map that helps the user understand and use it.

It can include:

- Title
- Legend
- Scale
- North arrow
- Coordinate information
- Source information
- Projection information
- Date
- Other map information

Before interpreting a map, examine its supporting information.

---

## Map Projection

Earth is approximately spherical, while a map is usually displayed on a flat surface.

A map projection is a method used to represent Earth's curved surface on a flat map.

Different projections can introduce different types of distortion.

Possible distortions include changes in:

- Shape
- Area
- Distance
- Direction

There is no single flat map projection that preserves every geographic property everywhere.

---

## Measuring Distance on a Map

Map scale can be used to convert a map distance into a real-world distance.

### Example

Suppose:

Map scale = 1:50,000

A measured distance on the map is:

2 cm

Then the ground distance is:

2 × 50,000 = 100,000 cm

Convert to kilometers:

100,000 cm = 1 km

Therefore:

2 cm on the map represents 1 km on the ground.

---

## Measuring Area

Maps and remote sensing images can be used to estimate the area of features.

Examples include:

- Lakes
- Forests
- Urban areas
- Agricultural fields
- Burned areas

If the area represented by one pixel is known, pixel counts can sometimes be used to estimate the total area of a feature.

---

## Image Maps vs. Thematic Maps

### Image Map

Displays measurements collected by a sensor as an image.

Examples:

- Satellite imagery
- Thermal imagery
- Radar imagery

### Thematic Map

Displays a particular subject or theme.

Examples:

- Land cover map
- Population map
- Elevation map
- Vegetation map

---

## Natural Color Maps

Natural-color imagery is designed to look similar to what the human eye sees.

It commonly uses visible:

- Red
- Green
- Blue

bands.

Natural-color imagery can make familiar features easier to recognize.

---

## False Color Maps

False-color imagery assigns spectral bands to colors that may not match their natural appearance.

This can make features easier to distinguish.

For example, vegetation can appear in colors that are very different from natural green.

The exact appearance depends on which spectral bands are assigned to the red, green, and blue display channels.

Always check the image legend or band information before interpreting false-color imagery.

---

## Radar Maps

Radar images display information derived from returned radar signals.

Radar imagery can reveal characteristics that may not be obvious in visible imagery.

Radar backscatter can be affected by:

- Surface roughness
- Moisture
- Material properties
- Geometry
- Viewing conditions

---

## Thermal Maps

Thermal imagery displays information related to emitted thermal radiation.

It can be used to study differences in surface temperature.

Examples include:

- Urban heat
- Water temperature differences
- Wildfire effects
- Surface temperature patterns

---

## Important Mapping Ideas

When looking at a remote sensing map or image, first ask:

1. What area does the map cover?
2. What type of data is being displayed?
3. What does the legend mean?
4. What is the map scale?
5. Which direction is north?
6. What are the units?
7. What do the colors or symbols represent?
8. What date was the data collected?
9. What sensor or data source was used?
10. What measurements can be made from the map?

# Key Memory Tricks

**Legend → What do the symbols mean?**

**Scale → How far?**

**Orientation → Which direction?**

**Coordinates → Where?**

**Contour lines → Same elevation/value**

**DEM → Digital elevation information**

**Large scale → More detail, smaller area**

**Small scale → Less detail, larger area**
