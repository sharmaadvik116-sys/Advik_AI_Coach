# Satellite Orbits and Remote Sensing

## What Is an Orbit?

An orbit is the path followed by an object as it travels around another object because of gravity.

Earth-observing satellites are placed into different orbits depending on what they need to observe.

The satellite's orbit affects:

- How high the satellite is above Earth
- How quickly it travels
- How often it can observe a location
- How large an area it can observe
- How the satellite appears to move relative to Earth

---

## Low Earth Orbit (LEO)

Low Earth Orbit, or LEO, is relatively close to Earth's surface.

LEO satellites generally:

- Travel at high speeds
- Complete an orbit relatively quickly
- Can provide detailed observations
- Are commonly used for Earth observation

Because the satellite is relatively close to Earth, instruments can obtain detailed measurements while covering a smaller area at a time than satellites at much higher altitudes.

---

## Medium Earth Orbit (MEO)

Medium Earth Orbit is higher than LEO and lower than high Earth orbits.

MEO is commonly used by satellite systems that need coverage over larger areas.

The orbital period is longer than for a typical LEO satellite.

---

## High Earth Orbit

High Earth orbits are much farther from Earth's surface.

A satellite at a greater altitude generally has:

- A longer orbital period
- A larger potential field of view
- A different relationship between its motion and Earth's rotation

---

## Polar Orbit

A polar orbit passes approximately over Earth's poles.

As Earth rotates underneath the satellite, the satellite can observe different parts of Earth's surface during successive passes.

Polar-orbiting satellites can therefore provide coverage over much of Earth's surface over time.

---

## Non-Polar Orbits

A non-polar orbit does not pass approximately over both poles.

The satellite's path may be designed to repeatedly observe particular regions or provide specific coverage.

---

## Geostationary Orbit

A geostationary satellite has a special orbit around Earth.

For a satellite to remain approximately above the same location on Earth's equator, it must:

- Orbit above the equator
- Travel in the same direction as Earth's rotation
- Have an orbital period matching Earth's rotation

From the ground, such a satellite appears to remain over approximately the same location.

Geostationary satellites are useful for continuously monitoring large areas.

---

## Geosynchronous Orbit

A geosynchronous satellite has an orbital period equal to Earth's rotation period.

A geosynchronous orbit does not necessarily appear fixed over one location.

A geostationary orbit is a special case of a geosynchronous orbit.

For an orbit to be geostationary, it must also be:

- Circular
- Above the equator
- Moving in the same direction as Earth's rotation

---

## Geostationary vs. Geosynchronous

| Feature | Geosynchronous | Geostationary |
|---|---|---|
| Orbital period matches Earth's rotation | Yes | Yes |
| Must be above equator | No | Yes |
| Must be circular | No | Yes |
| Appears fixed over one location | Not necessarily | Yes |

---

# Kepler's Third Law

Kepler's Third Law describes a relationship between the orbital period of an object and the size of its orbit.

In simplified form:

T² ∝ a³

Where:

- T = orbital period
- a = semimajor axis of the orbit

For circular orbits, the orbital radius can be used as the main distance from the center of Earth.

The important idea is:

**A satellite farther from Earth generally takes longer to complete one orbit.**

---

## Using the Orbital Relationship

For two satellites orbiting the same central body:

T₁² / a₁³ = T₂² / a₂³

This relationship can be used to compare orbital periods.

### Example

Suppose one satellite has an orbital size that is larger than another satellite's orbital size.

The satellite with the larger orbital size will have a longer orbital period.

---

## Orbit Height and Speed

For circular orbits, satellites in lower orbits generally move faster than satellites in higher orbits.

This is why a LEO satellite can complete an orbit much more quickly than a satellite in a much higher orbit.

Remember:

**Lower orbit → generally faster orbital speed → shorter orbital period**

**Higher orbit → generally slower orbital speed → longer orbital period**

---

# Why Orbit Matters in Remote Sensing

The orbit of a satellite affects the observations that the sensor can make.

### LEO

Useful for:

- Detailed Earth observations
- Mapping
- Repeated observations
- Imaging Earth's surface

### Polar orbit

Useful for:

- Broad coverage of Earth
- Observing different regions as Earth rotates underneath the satellite

### Geostationary orbit

Useful for:

- Continuous monitoring of a large region
- Weather observations
- Tracking rapidly changing atmospheric conditions

---

# Temporal Resolution and Orbits

A satellite's orbit affects how often it can observe a particular location.

This relates to temporal resolution.

Temporal resolution describes how frequently a sensor can collect observations of the same area.

A satellite designed for frequent observations may have an orbit and sensor system designed to provide appropriate revisit times.

---

# Important Distinction

Do not confuse:

### Orbit altitude

How far the satellite is above Earth.

### Spatial resolution

How much ground area is represented by each image pixel.

### Temporal resolution

How frequently the same area can be observed.

A satellite can have a high altitude but still have a sensor with a particular spatial resolution.

These are related to satellite system design, but they are not the same measurement.
